Toddler Language Development From 18 to 24 Months

Between 18 and 24 months, communication often grows from gestures and a few meaningful words toward a wider vocabulary and early two-word combinations, but the path is not identical for every child.

Language includes more than spoken words. Understanding, pointing, showing, taking turns, copying actions, making sounds, and using facial expression are all part of communication. A child may understand much more than they can say.

Milestones describe skills most children show by an age; they are not a strict weekly schedule. Prematurity, hearing, motor development, temperament, and exposure to one or more languages influence the visible pattern.

Communication around 18 months

Many children point to share interest, follow a simple direction without a gesture, look at a few pages with an adult, and try several words besides names for parents. Their pronunciation may be unclear, and one sound can represent a whole word.

Notice whether the word or gesture is used consistently and intentionally. “Ba” said while pointing to a ball can be a meaningful word even when only family understands it.

Changes approaching age two

Vocabulary often expands, gestures become more varied, and children begin combining two ideas: “more milk,” “mama go,” or “big truck.” They may point to pictures and body parts, copy words, and understand familiar one-step directions.

Children learning multiple languages can divide words across languages. Count the total communication across all languages rather than expecting the full vocabulary in each one. Mixing languages is a normal strategy, not confusion.

Language-building habits that fit daily life

  • Follow your toddler’s focus and name what they are already looking at.
  • Expand one step: if they say “dog,” answer, “Big dog running.”
  • Pause after a question or comment to leave room for a response.
  • Offer choices with words and objects: “Banana or yogurt?”
  • Repeat favorite books and songs rather than always seeking something new.

During chores, narrate only a few useful words: “Sock in,” “Door open,” “Water cold.” Short, repeated language is easier to connect with action than a constant stream of adult speech.

Books, pretend play, and conversation

Let the toddler turn pages and point. You do not need to finish the printed story. Name one picture, imitate a sound, or ask “Where is the bear?” and wait. In pretend play, model small phrases such as “Baby sleep” or “Soup hot.”

Avoid correcting pronunciation through repeated demands. Respond with the correct form naturally: the child says “tar,” and you answer, “Yes, a car.” Our reading guide has more ideas for shared books.

When to ask for an evaluation

Share concerns early if your toddler rarely gestures, does not seem to understand familiar language, has very few meaningful sounds or words, loses a skill, or does not begin combining words around age two. Hearing should be considered even after a normal newborn screen.

Your pediatrician may recommend hearing testing, a speech-language evaluation, developmental screening, or early-intervention services. Asking does not create a diagnosis; it gives you clearer information during a period when support can be especially useful.

Create more chances to communicate

Place a favorite object where it is visible but requires help, pause before opening a snack, or offer two shirts. Wait several seconds for any response: a look, reach, point, sound, sign, or word. Answer immediately and model a short phrase without requiring imitation. Keep a monthly note of new words, gestures, and consistent approximations. Share examples from every language used at home with the pediatrician; the total communication picture matters more than counting only one language.

Common questions

Do signs and gestures delay speech?

No. Gestures give a child a way to communicate and can be paired with spoken words. Say the word as you respond to the sign rather than withholding help until the child speaks.

Should I use flashcards?

Real interaction is usually more useful. Naming a shoe while putting it on or a spoon during lunch connects language with action, senses, and shared attention.

Is screen content educational for language?

Toddlers learn best from responsive people. If media is used, choose age-appropriate content, watch together, and connect what appears on screen with real objects and conversation.
